Corruption

June 15, 2012  ·  Roshan Nagekar  ·  5 min read

A king once announced in his kingdom, "Anyone who gets him  something that is really unique, which no one in this world has will be rewarded, but if its not unique, he will be hanged" A tailor came forward and gifted him an air jacket. He said that the jacket was invisible and was made up of air. The king proudly got up wore nothing but the jacket and proudly went on a ride in the kingdom. No one else, but a 12 year old kid came forward laughing out of his innocence and honesty saying "O king!! You are wearing absolutely nothing and are roaming naked giving your kingdom a full view of yourself." This is the end of the story. Don’t really know what happened with the kid, but pretty sure he dint live thereafter!!! Hope everyone got the right message….

Why This Story Still Resonates

The allegory above is a retelling of "The Emperor’s New Clothes," but the core of it maps almost perfectly onto how corruption survives in the real world. The king is not uniquely evil — he is simply a person in power who has been insulated from honest feedback. The tailor is not uniquely dishonest — he is someone who figured out that telling a powerful person what they want to hear is far more profitable than telling the truth. And everyone in the kingdom who stayed silent? They were just people calculating their personal risk.

That calculation is the engine of systemic corruption. Corruption does not persist because everyone involved is a villain. It persists because the incentive structures reward silence and punish honesty. In most corrupt systems, the person who speaks up — the child in the story — faces immediate, personal consequences, while those who maintain the fiction get to continue collecting their rewards. When the cost of honesty falls entirely on the individual and the benefit of silence is distributed across an entire system, silence becomes the rational choice for most people.

Accountability mechanisms exist precisely to change that math. Whistleblower protection laws, for example, are designed to lower the personal cost of speaking truth — to make it less likely that the honest child gets hanged. Right to Information (RTI) acts and Freedom of Information (FOIA) requests give ordinary citizens the legal right to ask governments hard questions and receive documented answers. Open government data initiatives — where budgets, contracts, and decisions are published publicly — make the emperor walk through the kingdom naked by default, rather than waiting for one brave child to point it out.

These are not perfect tools. RTI requests get stonewalled. Whistleblowers still face retaliation despite legal protections. Open data portals sometimes publish data in formats nobody can actually use. But they matter because they shift the structure slightly: they create friction for corruption, and they reduce the personal cost of accountability. The allegory’s king tests his subjects’ willingness to bring him something unique. Transparency mechanisms test a government’s willingness to be seen clearly. The ones that pass the test earn trust. The ones that don’t tell you everything you need to know.

What Can Individuals Do?

The story ends on a bleak note — the honest child probably did not survive. But that is not a reason to stay silent; it is a reason to build systems where honesty is safer. Here are concrete things individuals can do:

Vote with this in mind. Candidates who support strong whistleblower protections, independent oversight bodies, and genuine open-data policies are making structural commitments. Campaign promises about "fighting corruption" are cheap. Specific institutional reforms are not.

Use RTI and FOIA requests. These tools exist and most people never use them. If you want to know how a budget was spent, how a contract was awarded, or how a decision was made, file a request. The process is often straightforward, and even a denied request generates a paper trail that can be followed up.

Support institutions that create accountability. Independent journalism, non-partisan audit organizations, and civil society watchdogs do the work of the honest child at scale. They are worth financial support and public amplification when they get things right.

Hold local leadership accountable first. Systemic national corruption is hard to fight from the outside. Local corruption — in municipal contracting, school board decisions, local police oversight — is where individuals actually have leverage. Attend meetings, ask questions, document decisions.

Refuse to participate in small corruptions. This sounds obvious, but the king’s courtiers did not start by endorsing an imaginary jacket. Small normalizations accumulate. Pushing back on minor dishonesty in your own context, even at some personal cost, matters more than it might feel like it does.

The child in the story had no institutional protection and paid for their honesty. The goal of accountability infrastructure is to make sure the next honest child does not have to.
